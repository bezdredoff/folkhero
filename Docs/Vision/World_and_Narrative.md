# World and Narrative Bible

Status: v0.1 — locked narrative/world direction for pre-production.

## Historical frame

Folkhero is set in an alternate-history late-16th-century world based on the late Grand Duchy of Lithuania / early Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.

The Livonian War (1558–1583) is the main historical skeleton.

Real:
- states;
- places;
- rulers;
- wars;
- political structures;

may be used by name.

The game is not strict historical reconstruction. The supernatural is real and can change events significantly.

## Tone

The setting should feel historically grounded enough that supernatural divergence matters.

Avoid:
- generic pseudo-medieval fantasy;
- simplistic "peasants good / rulers bad" morality;
- treating folklore as decorative monster names.

Political actors may have understandable motives while still causing destructive outcomes.

## Folklore worldview

The supernatural world is plural and local.

Many spirits:
- are tied to places;
- follow old rules;
- respect boundaries;
- respond to rituals, agreements and taboo;
- are not automatically benevolent or evil.

Humans can create danger by breaking old agreements.

The world also contains:
- genuinely malicious spirits;
- curses;
- undead;
- corruption;
- predatory supernatural creatures.

## Home region

The starting landscape is inspired by the modern Berezinsky Biosphere Reserve / upper Berezina region.

Visual/ecological vocabulary:
- northern-central Belarus forest-marsh mosaic;
- river headwaters;
- springs;
- bogs;
- lakes;
- forests;
- villages;
- khutors;
- seasonal rain;
- snow;
- flooding.

## Inciting incident

Near the home village is a ritual place at a spring beside a marsh where a river begins.

The ritual maintains an old local agreement with a Bolotnik.

The Bolotnik is important to this place but not globally unique. Other swamps/forests/rivers have their own local spirits and agreements.

A new authority attempts to destroy or suppress the ritual place, treating it publicly as superstition, disorder or an obstacle.

The player characters resist.

The authority still destroys the place.

The agreement breaks.

The local supernatural balance worsens.

The heroes become politically unsafe/wanted and must leave.

## Wider revelation

The player initially believes the disaster is local.

Campaign understanding should develop roughly as:

1. This happened to our village.
2. Similar failures have already happened elsewhere.
3. The pattern is systematic.
4. Higher levels know the supernatural is real and are exploiting the consequences deliberately.

## The military supernatural program

Knowledge is tiered.

### Local level
Many soldiers, officials and minor administrators may genuinely believe:
- rituals are backward superstition;
- local customs obstruct administration;
- resistance is ordinary lawlessness.

### Higher level
Senior political/military leadership knows supernatural forces are real.

The broad policy is sanctioned by the ruler.

Primary strategic motive:
- exploit supernatural disruption/power for war and territorial advantage.

Secondary motives may include:
- centralization;
- resource extraction;
- infrastructure;
- administrative unification.

### Operational organization
A dedicated military service/organization runs practical operations.

Character:
- primarily military;
- pragmatic;
- researchers/occult specialists subordinate to military goals.

It can contain:
- officers;
- engineers;
- occult specialists;
- researchers;
- fortified experiments;
- seized relics;
- coerced local experts;
- experimental units.

The organization may hide methods and consequences even from political leadership while still pursuing a sanctioned strategic goal.

Its final name/structure is not yet locked.

## Player-character origin

The player characters are simple archetypes from the same home community, not pre-authored celebrity heroes and not members of a monster-hunting order.

Working fiction:

### Warrior
A returned/retired soldier or veteran.

### Vedun
A folk-magic practitioner/hermit from a nearby khutor.

Their personal biography should stay light enough to support player identification.

## Displacement and community

A central theme is:

**Home is not lost. Home is inaccessible.**

Many people remain in the original village.

A core group of service/community NPCs leaves with or around the heroes.

These familiar people become the recurring hub community:
- craftsmen;
- merchants;
- ornament specialists;
- support/service NPCs;
- recurring social/narrative figures.

The community moves through regional hubs and creates continuity between acts.

Some displaced NPCs may be able to travel home more safely than the heroes and carry:
- letters;
- news;
- items;
- rumors.

The heroes cannot safely return because of their political status and resistance to the new order.

## Return-home gameplay

A clandestine return may be possible earlier and should be dangerous.

Potential systemic variables:
- PoliticalHeat;
- LocalSupport;
- checkpoint density;
- patrols;
- smuggler access;
- disguise/safe-route availability.

The final campaign payoff is a safe return to a changed home.

Only two major routes are needed:

### Route A — Patronage
Gain protection from an influential political/military side able to guarantee safety.

### Route B — Gray political change
War, shifting borders or a change in power makes return possible without a clean vindication.

Neither route should magically restore the old status quo.

## Campaign structure

First full game target:
- 5 acts;
- 3 major reusable regions;
- ~8–10 hours mandatory main story;
- ~12–15 hours typical first playthrough;
- ~3–4 authored main quests per act.

### Act I — Home and exile
- establish village/community;
- ritual/pact;
- authority conflict;
- sacred site destroyed;
- heroes become fugitives;
- first displacement.

### Act II — Wider crisis
- enter outside region;
- discover similar supernatural failures are older and widespread.

### Act III — War administration
- Polotsk-region focus;
- military program becomes visible;
- political/supernatural exploitation is no longer deniable.

### Act IV — Escalation
- revisit altered familiar places;
- stronger war pressure;
- seasonal/world-state changes;
- consequences of previous decisions become visible.

### Act V — Return conditions
- central confrontation;
- choose/earn one of the two return routes;
- return to changed home;
- epilogue reflects campaign-level world state.

## Quest philosophy

Main quest:
**fixed story + variable journey**.

Fixed:
- narrative objective;
- important NPC;
- authored anchor;
- major choice/outcome.

Variable:
- generated route;
- weather;
- encounter mix;
- optional objectives;
- world-state conditions;
- loot.

Repeatable content can use systemic quest grammar more aggressively.

## Consequence scope

Three horizons:

### Local
Immediate quest/area consequences.

### Regional
Persistent changes to:
- patrols;
- routes;
- enemy mixes;
- NPCs;
- merchants;
- side quests;
- visuals;
- safety.

### Campaign
Only about 3–5 major decisions should substantially affect final conditions and epilogue.

Avoid huge bespoke branching trees.

## Major historical figures

Use major rulers mostly through:
- decrees;
- letters;
- rumors;
- envoys;
- campaign orders;
- policies.

Do not turn the game into a dialogue-heavy court RPG.

Hack-and-slash remains the primary experience.
